# How competitors present the equipment they work on

Reviewed 14 October 2026 for the homepage section “The equipment we work on.”

The question was layout, not copy. Nothing below is for reuse as words or pictures. The notes describe how each site is organized, what is easy to scan, and what FSC should not repeat.

A site that publishes FSC’s phone number was set aside and is not treated as a competitor.

A Kissimmee “gate operator repair” page from a templated lead site was opened and dropped. It repeats brand names and city copy and does not show a useful equipment layout.

## What each site does

**FDC** splits the offer two ways. The services page is a short set of blocks: access control, gates, maintenance plans, and a few adjacent trades, each with install / repair / maintain in the same breath. The security-gates page then splits the hardware by how it moves: sliding (needs a back track), cantilever (no ground track), swing (when there is no runback), then lift, tilt, barrier, and crash as a second group. Operators are a separate explanation tied to cycle count, not mixed into the leaf. That split is the useful part. The pages also publish price ranges, a license-plate example, long city lists, and filler industry cards. Those are not useful.

**Central Florida Gate Services** uses a six-item service row: gate automation, custom fencing, telephone entry, mobile welding, access controls, maintenance and repair. Telephone entry is its own item, which is the right call. The rest of the page is a photo gallery of jobs (“Examples”) with almost no explanation of the equipment. Easy to glance at, hard to learn from, and it depends on job photos FSC does not have.

**Kings Access Control** is the clearest local scan. Seven icon tiles in a row: gate services (swing, slide, barrier, pedestrian), access control (cards, fobs), cameras, maintenance agreements, and a few non-equipment items. One line under each icon says what the tile is for. No geometry lesson. The weakness is that the tiles do not link to a page about that equipment, and cameras are described only as “clarity.”

**Xpress Gates Repair** is a long list of repair types: automatic gate, sliding, swing, opener, motor, then access control, phone entry, and keypads as their own blocks. Phone entry and keypads are correctly separated from the leaf. A brand row names the manufacturers they work on, in text, with no certification claim on the home page. The list is too long to scan, and several blocks say the same thing.

**D&A Gate and Access Control** sorts first by commercial vs residential vs service, then mentions telephone entry and intercom as things they troubleshoot. Brands are a sentence (“all of the major brands”), not a strip. Little equipment structure. Mostly a contact page.

**Gate Tech** leads with fabrication and maintenance plans, then a logo row of manufacturers the shop is factory-trained on. The logos do the “what we work on” job in one glance. The page around them is neighborhood copy, reviews, and photos, not an equipment catalog.

**KMG Fence** is the best local *explanation* of gate type, in stacked sections rather than cards: sliding and cantilever, heavy-duty swing, then an “access control integration” group of keypads and card readers, telephone entry and intercoms, and loop detectors. Barrier arms appear in the FAQ, not in the grid. Each type gets a one-line reason it fits a site. No prices on this page. No icons. No jump to a matching service page per type.

**LiftMaster** (national) uses a solution grid, not a photo grid: commercial gate operators, access control, and accessories. The operator page then splits again by motion: industrial, barrier, swing, slide, specialty. One idea per tile, short label, and a destination. That is the cleanest catalog pattern in the set. It is a product catalog, so the tiles sell a model family rather than install / repair / maintenance.

**DoorKing** (national) draws one commercial entry and numbers the parts: backup power, gate operators, telephone entry, lane barriers, card readers, keypads, and others. Under the drawing, four groups repeat the same split in words: visitor communication, heavy-duty operators, access-control devices, traffic and parking. The numbered drawing is thorough and slow. The four groups underneath are the part worth keeping: operators, telephone entry, credentials, and barrier arms are different jobs.

## What works best

1. **One card per equipment type, named the way a property manager says it.** Slide, swing, vertical lift, and barrier arm stay separate. Operators stay separate from the leaf. Keypads stay separate from the call box. Cameras stay their own card. FDC, KMG, LiftMaster, and DoorKing all make this split. Kings and Central Florida Gate Services stop at “gates” and “access,” which is harder to match to a lane.

2. **One plain sentence on why that type exists.** Not a geometry diagram and not a paragraph. KMG and FDC both earn the click by saying when the type fits (runback, arc, overhead, a lane that only needs an arm). The current FSC diagrams teach that idea and then hide it inside a navy drawing.

3. **The work, in three words, on the card.** Install, repair, maintenance. FDC puts all three in one service block. Xpress spreads them across a dozen headings. The short form scans; the long form does not.

4. **A real link to the page about that equipment.** LiftMaster and FDC send each type somewhere. Kings and the FSC diagrams do not. The link should name the service page the click lands on.

5. **A quiet strip of real manufacturer marks under the grid.** Gate Tech and Xpress both answer “can you work on what we already have?” with brands, not with another essay. A static row is readable. A moving row is not. No “authorized” or “certified” line unless that status is on file.

6. **Icon, not a job photo and not an exploded drawing.** Kings’ icon tiles are the fastest local pattern. DoorKing’s numbered site plan is the most complete and the least scannable. The FSC navy diagrams sit in the second group.

## What not to bring over

- Prices, ranges, or “starting at.”
- Round-the-clock claims.
- License-plate readers, plate cameras, or “AI camera” language. One FDC gate page uses plate recognition as an example. Leave it out.
- Job-photo galleries and stock photography. Central Florida Gate Services and Gate Tech depend on them. FSC does not have a cleared photo set.
- Competitor names, rankings, or comparison lines on the FSC page.
- City-list blocks and street addresses inside the equipment section.
- Certification or “factory authorized” claims. Gate Tech states factory training because that is their claim. FSC’s strip stays “equipment we install and service,” which is the line already approved for these logos.

## What the redesign uses

Patterns only, rewritten in FSC’s voice and pointed at FSC’s existing service pages:

- Eight cards: slide gates, swing gates, vertical lift gates, barrier arms, operators, keypads and access control, call boxes and intercoms, cameras.
- A line icon on each card, navy tile, red used only for the part that moves or the part you look at.
- One benefit sentence, then the labels Install, Repair, and Maintenance.
- One link per card: gate types to `/services/security-gate-systems`, operators to `/services/gate-automation`, keypads and call boxes to `/services/access-control`, cameras to `/services/video-surveillance`.
- The eleven owner-confirmed manufacturer logos, shown once in a static strip under the cards, with the existing heading “Equipment we install and service.”

The line diagrams stay on the service and industry pages, where a single drawing can explain one piece of hardware. They are no longer the homepage catalog.
